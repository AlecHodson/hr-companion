Conduct

Disciplinary & Investigation

Covers the full run from a raised allegation through investigation, optional suspension, disciplinary hearing, and outcome.

Flag — worth raising with HR/Legal now: our own handbook says the Company can discipline or dismiss without following this procedure at all for employees with under 24 months' service, or for workers. From 1 January 2027, employees gain unfair dismissal protection after just 6 months' service. Relying on the handbook's 24-month short-service exception for anyone between 6 and 24 months' service will no longer be safe once that change lands — worth reviewing the handbook wording before the end of 2026.

Consider informal resolution first

Ask whether this can be resolved with a conversation, clarified expectations, or support — before starting a formal process. Not every issue needs the full machinery.

Changing — not yet law: Acas's July 2026 draft Code of Practice would require your formal invite letter to state what informal steps were tried, or explain why none were appropriate. Consultation runs to 23 September 2026; the current 2015 Code doesn't require this yet, but it's good practice to start documenting it now.

Investigate

Appoint an investigator (not someone who'll later chair the disciplinary hearing). Gather evidence and witness statements before speaking to the employee where possible.

Use the Witness Statement template for anyone who saw or heard something relevant — factual detail only, no opinion.
Our policy: the Investigation Meeting is treated as informal, so there's no company right to be accompanied at this stage — only under-18s can bring a parent/guardian. Save the right to a companion for the formal disciplinary hearing itself.

Decide whether suspension is necessary

Suspension is not a disciplinary sanction and must be on full pay. It should only be used where it's genuinely necessary — for example, to protect evidence, other staff, or children in your care.

Flag: case law already treats suspension as not a neutral act — it can itself damage trust and must be justified. The 2026 Acas draft goes further, framing suspension as a last resort that must be documented and kept under active review, not a default response to any allegation. Don't suspend as a matter of routine.

Hold the investigation meeting

This is fact-finding, not disciplinary — make that explicit to the employee. Use the Investigation Meeting Notes template, read the wellbeing statement, and don't decide the outcome in the room.

Decide afterwards: no further action / performance improvement plan / further investigation / refer to a disciplinary hearing.

Invite to a disciplinary hearing (if conduct route)

Set out the allegations in writing, disclose the evidence gathered, confirm the right to be accompanied by a colleague or trade union rep, and be clear if gross misconduct — and therefore summary dismissal — is in play.

Current law: the statutory right to be accompanied applies to any formal disciplinary or grievance hearing. Refusing a reasonable request, or failing to allow a companion to play their proper role, is itself a legal risk independent of the underlying issue.

Decide the outcome and confirm in writing

No action, a warning, or dismissal — the sanction must be proportionate and consistent with how similar cases have been handled before. Always confirm the right of appeal.

Changing: from 1 January 2027, employees gain unfair dismissal protection after 6 months' service instead of 2 years (Employment Rights Act 2025). Anyone employed on or before 1 July 2026 will already have that qualifying service by the change date. This makes getting the process right for shorter-service staff far more important, starting now.

Check with Mentor and HR before you deliver it

Before any disciplinary outcome — a warning or a dismissal — is delivered to the employee, the draft letter and the reasoning behind it should go to Mentor (our external employment law advisers) and Karen Tate (HR Business Partner) to confirm no risk has been identified. This is a checkpoint, not a formality — it's the point where a process issue or a discrimination risk gets caught before it becomes irreversible.

Don't skip this even under time pressure. Once an outcome letter is delivered, it's much harder to unwind than it is to pause and get sign-off first.

Close out

Send the outcome letter (within your policy's stated timescale), send a copy of the meeting minutes, update the personnel file, arrange any training or support agreed, and set a review date if applicable.

Escalate before you go further if:

  • The allegation involves a child, safeguarding, or a regulatory body may need informing
  • Dismissal is a realistic outcome
  • The employee has raised discrimination, a disability, or a protected characteristic alongside the conduct issue
  • You're the subject of the complaint, or too close to it to run the process impartially
Performance

Probation & Performance

The non-conduct route: new starters finding their feet, and existing staff whose standard of work has slipped. Keep this separate from the Disciplinary process unless conduct (not capability) is genuinely the issue.

Flag: the Employee Handbook doesn't have a dedicated probation policy section — probation length and terms sit in the contract of employment instead. Check the individual's contract for their probation length before relying on any date in this section.

Set clear objectives from day one

For a new starter — especially into a management role — a structured plan with weekly or monthly milestones gives you something concrete to review against, rather than a vague sense that things "aren't quite right."

Raise concerns early and informally where you can

A quick conversation is often enough. Where something needs to be on record but doesn't yet justify a full performance process, a letter of concern flags the standard expected without escalating to a formal warning.

Hold a formal performance meeting if concerns continue

Discuss the specific standard not being met, give the employee a proper chance to explain, and decide whether a Performance Improvement Plan is the right next step.

Put a Performance Improvement Plan in place

Specific tasks/standards required, how success will be measured, training or support provided, and review dates. Make sure the employee has genuinely had the chance to comment.

If it's a probationer — hold probation review meetings

Review against the objectives you set at the start. Three outcomes: pass, extend, or fail. Give the employee the chance to respond before you decide.

Flag: from 1 January 2027, unfair dismissal protection starts at 6 months' service. If a probation period runs close to or past that point, failing someone at the end of probation needs the same rigour as any other dismissal — don't treat "still on probation" as a safe shortcut once this change lands.

Close out

Confirm the outcome in writing either way — pass, extend, fail (which is a dismissal and needs the same care as any other), or move to a PIP if performance issues emerge post-probation.

Escalate before you go further if:

  • You're not sure whether an issue is conduct (disciplinary) or capability (performance) — the wrong route can undermine the whole process
  • The employee has raised a health condition, disability, or personal circumstance that might explain the performance issue
  • Failing probation or ending a PIP in dismissal for anyone approaching 6 months' service, once the January 2027 change is in force
Concerns

Grievance

Any concern, problem or complaint an employee raises about their employment — whether or not they call it "a grievance."

Try to resolve it informally first

This isn't just a future nice-to-have — it's already our current policy. Where possible, the employee should try to settle a grievance informally with their manager at the earliest opportunity, before the full formal procedure is invoked. A quick, honest conversation resolves far more than people expect, and it's far less disruptive to the working relationship than going straight to a formal process.

If you're the subject of the concern, or too close to it, point the employee to another manager or a more senior person instead of trying to handle it yourself.
Changing — not yet law: the 2026 Acas draft would go further and bring informal resolution into the statutory Code itself, and it also nudges employees toward shorter, more specific written grievances that state the outcome they want. Not yet in force, but the direction of travel is clear — see the Employee Conflict & Mediation page for practical ways to do this well.

Recognise it — don't wait to be asked

An employee doesn't need to invoke the formal procedure by name for it to count as a grievance. If informal resolution hasn't worked, or isn't appropriate, treat it as a formal grievance from here.

Choose who handles it

Never the person the grievance is about. Ideally someone senior to them, and — since there's a right of appeal — leave someone more senior again available to hear that appeal.

Investigate every point raised

Interview witnesses and anyone the grievance is against. Respond to each point individually — you can uphold some points and not others.

One person's word against another is not a reason to avoid deciding — form a view and show your reasonable grounds for it.

Decide and confirm the outcome in writing

Send documentation to a mentor/second reviewer before issuing the outcome. Written outcome within 5 working days of the hearing, per our template — confirm the right of appeal.

Escalate immediately if the grievance involves:

  • Discrimination, harassment or victimisation related to a protected characteristic
  • Bullying by, or a complaint about, a manager or senior staff member
  • Safeguarding, whistleblowing, or a possible criminal matter
  • An employee who is currently signed off sick with stress or a mental health condition
Before it's formal

Conflict & Mediation

Most workplace friction never needs to become a grievance or a disciplinary. This is how to catch it earlier and resolve it well — for individual disputes and for a team that's just not gelling.

Spot it early

Conflict rarely announces itself as "conflict." Watch for: repeated minor complaints about the same colleague, a room that's gone quiet or tense, people avoiding each other's shifts, an uptick in short-notice absence from one team, or the same names coming up in every supervision.

Your Wellbeing Cafe check-ins and regular Staff Supervision sessions are exactly where this usually surfaces first — a question like "any tensions between rooms or staff members?" is there for a reason. Don't let a "no" go unquestioned if the atmosphere says otherwise.

Talk to each person separately first

Before bringing people together, hear each side on their own. Stay curious rather than judgemental — you're gathering understanding, not building a case.

  • What's actually happened, in specific terms (not "they're always difficult")
  • What they'd like to be different going forward
  • Whether they're open to a joint conversation, or need more time first

Decide if mediation is the right tool

Mediation works well for personality clashes, communication breakdowns, and disagreements about how work gets done. It is not the right tool everywhere.

Don't mediate — escalate instead — if: the issue involves discrimination, harassment, bullying, safeguarding, or a significant power imbalance (e.g. a junior member of staff and their manager). Mediation assumes both parties are roughly equal and both acting in good faith; where that's not true, it can do real harm by treating a rights issue as "a difference of opinion."

Prepare a session plan before you invite anyone

Think through the background, the key issues, and what a good outcome looks like before the invite goes out — not in the room. This is also your last checkpoint to confirm mediation is genuinely the right tool for this situation.

Run the mediation session

Use a neutral facilitator — someone with no stake in the outcome and not the direct line manager of either party if you can avoid it. Structure it loosely like this:

  • Brief separate check-ins immediately before, to confirm both still want to go ahead
  • Set ground rules together at the start: one person speaks at a time, no interruptions, listen to understand rather than to rebut
  • Focus forward — what needs to change for this to work day-to-day — rather than re-litigating who was right
  • End with specific, agreed actions and a date to check back in
Keep notes light-touch and forward-focused — they're a record of what was agreed, not a case file. Make clear to both parties that these notes won't be used in a disciplinary or grievance process.

Follow up — don't just close the file

Check in with both people a couple of weeks later, separately. Agreements made in the room don't always survive contact with a busy shift — a quick follow-up catches that early.

Prevent it recurring with a staff charter

Where friction is a team-wide pattern rather than a one-off, get the team together to agree a short, plain-language charter: how we speak to each other, how we raise a concern, how we handle disagreement, what we do when someone's having a bad day. It works because the team wrote it, not because a manager handed it down.

  • Keep it short — 5 to 8 lines people can actually remember, not a policy document
  • Everyone in the room contributes at least one line
  • Display it somewhere visible in the room, not filed away
  • Revisit it periodically — a Wellbeing Cafe session is a natural moment to check it's still working

Move to a formal process instead of mediation if:

  • Discrimination, harassment, or a protected characteristic is involved
  • There's a significant power imbalance between the two people
  • Safeguarding is or could be involved
  • One party refuses to engage, or mediation has already been tried once for the same issue without success
Requests

Flexible Working

The statutory right to request a change to hours, times, or place of work.

Flag — our own documents disagree with each other: the Employee Handbook correctly states employees can make 2 flexible working requests in any rolling 12-month period — that's the current law and it's right. But the standalone Flexible Working Request Form still asks the employee to confirm they've made "not more than 1 request... in the past 12 months." As written, that form could cause a manager to wrongly reject a valid second request. Worth getting the form corrected to match the handbook.

Receive the request

Any employee can make a statutory request from their first day — there's no minimum service requirement.

Current law: day-one right since 6 April 2024. Up to 2 statutory requests per rolling 12 months. The request must be in writing and confirm it's made under section 80F of the Employment Rights Act 1996.

Acknowledge and prepare to meet

You have a maximum of 2 months from receipt to give a final decision, including any appeal — unless you and the employee agree in writing to extend it.

Hold the meeting and decide

You must consult with the employee before refusing a request — that's what this meeting is for. You can only refuse on one or more of the 8 statutory business grounds, and you must show your reasoning, not just tick a box.

Changing — not yet law: the Employment Rights Act 2025 will add a "reasonableness" test for refusals and require you to explain why a refusal is reasonable in the circumstances, not just cite a ground. Expected 2027; still in consultation as of mid-2026.

Handle any appeal

Whoever hears the appeal should not have been involved in the original decision.

Escalate before refusing if:

  • The request relates to a disability, pregnancy, or caring responsibilities — a refusal could also be an indirect discrimination claim, which isn't capped the way a flexible working claim is
  • You're not confident the ground you're relying on is genuinely made out and evidenced
Wellbeing

Absence & Return to Work

Sickness-related absence, from day one of the absence through to a return-to-work conversation.

Absence is reported

Our policy requires the employee to notify you personally — not by text, email, or via a colleague — no later than 30 minutes before their scheduled start time on the first day of absence.

Self-certification (our absence form) covers absences of 7 calendar days or fewer. Beyond 7 days, a GP fit note is required, submitted as soon as possible and renewed before the current one expires.

Statutory Sick Pay applies from day one

Changed 6 April 2026: SSP is now payable from the first qualifying day of sickness — the old 3 "waiting days" are gone. The lower earnings limit has also been scrapped, so eligibility no longer depends on how much someone earns. SSP is paid at whichever is lower: the flat statutory rate (£123.25/week for 2026/27) or 80% of average weekly earnings. If any absence paperwork or manager guidance still references a 4th-day start or a minimum earnings threshold, it's out of date.

Hold a return-to-work interview — every time

Our policy requires this after every absence, not just long ones. Discuss the reason, whether the employee is confirming they're fit to return, any GP recommendations, and whether there's an ongoing health issue that might need reasonable adjustments. You decide whether the absence is authorised — the onus is on the employee to satisfy you there was a genuine medical reason.

Flag: if the absence was due to work-related stress, our return-to-work template requires a stress risk assessment — don't skip this even if the employee says they're fine now.

Review the absence record against the real trigger point

Our policy's trigger is more than 3 occasions of absence (of any length) in a rolling 6-month period — reaching it starts an investigation and disciplinary action may follow.

Fixed: the return-to-work interview notes generator on this page now checks against this 6-month trigger, not the "3 periods in 3 months" figure the old paper template used — the two are aligned. If you're still working from a printed copy of the old form, replace it with the generated version.

Decide next steps

Options range from an informal supportive chat through to formal absence/capability management, a medical report (with consent), or in serious health-and-safety cases, a medical suspension on full pay. If the absence relates to a disability or long-term condition, this shifts into the Equality Act's reasonable adjustments duty — that's a conversation for HR before you act.

Escalate before starting formal absence management if:

  • There's an underlying disability or long-term health condition
  • The absence is pregnancy-related or during/shortly after maternity leave
  • Work itself may be a contributing cause (stress, an incident, a workplace injury)
Pregnancy & family

Pregnancy & Family Leave

From the moment an employee tells you they're pregnant or adopting, through risk assessment, leave, pay, and return to work — plus the other family-related leave types in the handbook.

Employee tells you they're pregnant or adopting

Congratulate them, and start the paper trail. There's no minimum service requirement — maternity/adoption leave is a day-one right.

Carry out a risk assessment — and keep reviewing it

Required by law for any new or expectant mother, and for anyone returning to work while breastfeeding. Cover manual handling, standing/fatigue, chemical or infection exposure, temperature, and working alone — all common hazards in a nursery setting. If a significant risk can't be removed, you must offer suitable alternative work, or suspend the employee on full pay until it's safe.

Confirm maternity/adoption leave and pay in writing

Once you have the MAT B1 (or adoption matching certificate) and a proposed start date, respond in writing within 28 calendar days confirming the leave dates and pay entitlement.

Current law/policy: 52 weeks' leave (26 ordinary + 26 additional, no gap between them); SMP/SAP payable for up to 39 weeks — 90% of average weekly earnings for the first 6 weeks, then the lower of £194.32/week (2026/27 rate) or 90% of average earnings for the remaining 33 weeks. To qualify for SMP/SAP the employee needs 26 weeks' continuous service by the qualifying week and average earnings at or above the Lower Earnings Limit (£129/week, 2026/27) — note this is a different, separate test from Statutory Sick Pay's day-one/no-earnings-limit rules.

Antenatal & adoption appointments

Pregnant employees get reasonable paid time off for antenatal care (after the first appointment, you can ask for evidence). Partners get unpaid time off to accompany, capped at 6.5 hours per appointment, up to 2 appointments. Main adopters get paid time off for up to 5 adoption appointments (6.5 hours each); partners get unpaid time off for up to 2.

Keeping in Touch (KIT) days, if used

Up to 10 days' work during leave without losing SMP/SAP — paid at normal rate. Neither side is obliged to offer or agree to them.

Return to work

No notice needed if returning on the originally agreed date. Returning early needs 8 weeks' notice from the employee. The right is to return to the same job after Ordinary Leave, and to the same job (or a similar one on no less favourable terms if that's not reasonably practicable) after Additional Leave.

Escalate before you act if:

  • A risk assessment identifies a hazard you can't remove or work around with alternative duties
  • An employee is dismissed, made redundant, or treated less favourably at any point during or shortly after pregnancy, maternity, adoption or shared parental leave — the protection here is some of the strongest in UK employment law
  • An employee wants to combine or convert leave types (e.g. maternity into shared parental leave)
Development

Training Agreements

Funding an employee's external training, with a fair repayment clause if they leave shortly afterwards.

Agree it in writing before training starts

The signed agreement is what makes any later repayment a lawful deduction from wages, rather than an unauthorised one under the Employment Rights Act 1996. Get it signed before the course begins, not after.

Set a repayment scale proportionate to the cost

Our template scales the minimum commitment period by cost (6 / 12 / 18 months) and tapers the repayment as time passes — a court or tribunal is far more likely to enforce a proportionate, tapering clause than a flat "repay it all" demand.

Build in the fairness exceptions

Our template already waives repayment for redundancy or medical incapacity — keep these, they're what stops the clause looking punitive.

Offboarding

Resignations & Leavers

Confirming a resignation and closing things out cleanly.

Get the resignation in writing

If someone resigns verbally, ask them to confirm it in writing (an email is fine) — it's the trigger for their notice period and last working day.

Confirm acceptance and the practicalities

Confirm the last working day, final pay date, P45 timing, return of company property, and system access cut-off. Remind them of any post-employment restrictions in their contract.

Training agreement check

If the employee has an active Training Agreement with a repayment clause, check whether it's triggered and calculate any deduction before the final payslip is run.

Escalate if:

  • The resignation follows a grievance, disciplinary process, or any complaint of discrimination or harassment — this could be a constructive dismissal risk
  • The employee resigns while pregnant, on family leave, or shortly after returning from it
Support

Staff Wellbeing & Benefits

The support structure available to staff, and the benefit rules managers get asked about most.

Spotting when someone might need support

You're not there to diagnose — you're there to notice, listen, and signpost. Watch for changes from someone's normal behaviour: withdrawal, irritability, tearfulness, fatigue, poor concentration, or a change in sleep or social activity. Regular one-to-ones are what make these changes visible early.

When you do have the conversation: ask open questions, don't work from a script, and focus on what's changed for them. Assess whether the level of concern is high, moderate, or low — that judgement shapes what happens next.

Respond according to the level of concern

High risk — immediate action; get support involved straight away, don't sit on it. Moderate risk — actively support the person in seeking help (GP, EAP, occupational health). Low risk — a supportive conversation about adjustments at work is often enough.

Mentor EAP is the confidential third-party service available to all staff for therapy and mental health support — you can point people to it directly, it doesn't need to go through you.

Use the wellbeing structure already in place

Every nursery has trained Mental Health First Aiders in the senior management team, and a WellCare Ambassador — a team member with a discretionary annual allowance to run wellbeing initiatives, agreed with the nursery manager. Ambassadors must have passed probation and be committed to staying at the nursery.

Run Wellbeing Cafe check-ins

A structured 1:1 conversation to surface how the team is really doing — atmosphere, tension between rooms, whether people feel able to raise concerns, and what would make things better. Useful after a difficult period, a run of leavers, or just as a periodic culture check.

Staff childcare discount — what managers get asked

Flag — two versions of this FAQ are in circulation and they don't agree. The undated original says a child can attend nursery while a parent is absent "for any reason" at full rate, and says the discount stops entirely during maternity leave. The Q4 2025 update narrows the full-rate trigger to sick absence specifically, and reverses the maternity leave answer — under the update, the discount continues during maternity leave. Use the Q4 2025 update below as the current position, and get the older document taken out of circulation — a manager working from the old version could wrongly charge full fees to someone on maternity leave, which is a poor look given how strongly the law protects staff during maternity leave.
Current rules (Q4 2025 update):
• Discounted rate applies while the parent is at work.
• Off sick? Child can still attend, but at the full rate.
• On maternity leave? Child can still attend at the discounted rate.
• Pre-booked annual leave (agreed by end of the previous calendar year) with child off nursery: no charge. Record the absence on Famly for both parent and child.
• Annual leave not pre-agreed in time, with child still attending: chargeable at the discounted rate.
• Appointment during booked leave, child can't come: an ad-hoc session can be booked, but at full fees.
• Funded hours: treated the same as any other funded child — subject to place availability at that nursery.

Escalate if:

  • Someone discloses a mental health crisis, thoughts of self-harm, or a safeguarding concern about a child — this needs immediate action through the right channel, not a wait-and-see approach
  • A childcare discount dispute overlaps with a grievance, or the parent is currently on maternity/family leave and disputes what they've been charged
Safer recruitment

Recruitment & Safer Onboarding

Getting a new starter safely into post — this is one of the most heavily regulated processes in childcare, and the rules just got stricter.

Flag — this whole area changed under you in September 2026. Our recruitment documents are dated October 2025, which pre-dates the new EYFS statutory framework edition that took effect 1 September 2026. Two changes matter most: (1) at least one written reference is now required before recruitment — a verbal reference alone, even if verified and satisfactory, is no longer enough on its own; and (2) no one — including students and volunteers — can start, even under supervision, until their enhanced DBS and barred list check have both actually been received, not just requested. Worth a full review of the recruitment pack against the current EYFS wording.

Application & shortlisting

Standard application form, open to all regardless of protected characteristics. Capture education, full work history (most recent first), and two referee contacts at this stage — one should be the current or most recent employer.

References — written, not just verbal

Use the Early Years Employment Reference Template, which asks the referee directly about reason for leaving, whether they'd re-employ, and any safeguarding concerns or DBS referrals. It should only record facts, not opinions, on any safeguarding-related answer.

Current rule: at least one written reference is required before recruitment, from the applicant's current/most recent employer, training provider or education setting — completed by someone with proper authority, never a family member, and never an open "to whom it may concern" reference. If the candidate isn't currently employed, verify their most recent relevant period of employment instead.

Employment gaps

Any gap in employment history needs a written explanation from the candidate — this is standard safer-recruitment practice, not something you need to feel awkward asking about.

Self-disclosure & DBS

Early years roles are exempt from the Rehabilitation of Offenders Act, so candidates must complete the Self Disclosure Form covering unspent and relevant spent convictions/cautions, and confirm they're not on the barred list. This sits alongside — not instead of — the enhanced DBS and barred list check.

Flag: no one can start work or volunteering, even supervised, until both the enhanced DBS certificate and the barred list check have actually been received. This now applies to students and volunteers too, following the removal of the old "supervision exemption."

Identity verification & the file

Take and file a verified photograph, and record how and when identity was checked — this feeds the Single Central Record, which Ofsted will check directly.

Ongoing suitability doesn't stop at day one

The new EYFS edition expects an ongoing staff suitability declaration process, not just a one-off check at recruitment. Good news: our Staff Supervision Form already asks exactly this — "are there any changes in your circumstances that may affect your suitability to work with children?" — at every supervision session. Make sure supervisions are actually happening on schedule; that's what makes this requirement real rather than theoretical.

Apprentices vs. unqualified practitioners — don't mix these up in ratio planning. Apprentices (apprenticeship contract) must not be counted in EYFS staffing ratios or staffing numbers, and must not hold key children — they're in training, with protected training time, and can be moved between rooms at the Nursery Manager's request to further that training. Unqualified Nursery Assistants, by contrast, can be counted in ratios where appropriate and can hold key children if the Nursery Manager judges them suitable. Getting this wrong isn't just an admin error — it's a ratio compliance risk. Managers can depart from this split only with their Area Manager's prior agreement.
Flag — two near-identical room leadership roles: "Room Senior / 3rd in Charge" and "Room Senior" describe almost the same job — leading a room, mentoring junior practitioners, EYFS planning, safeguarding — under slightly different titles and with different reporting lines shown (one reports only to Deputy Manager, the other to "Deputy Manager or Senior Practitioner"). Worth checking whether these are meant to be the same role documented twice, or genuinely different roles that need clearer differentiation.

Bullying & Harassment

What constitutes harassment, the types covered, and the manager’s duties when a complaint is raised.

Definition

Harassment is any unwanted conduct of a sexual nature that creates a hostile, degrading, humiliating or offensive environment.

Policy: “Any unwanted conduct of a sexual nature that creates a hostile, degrading, humiliating or offensive environment.” (Employee Handbook – Conduct and Standards)

Types of Harassment

• Sexual harassment (including third‑party harassment)
• Conduct that breaches the zero‑tolerance stance on sexual harassment

Policy: “Zero‑tolerance; any breach may be treated as gross misconduct and lead to dismissal without notice.” (Employee Handbook – Conduct and Standards)

Manager Action When a Complaint Is Raised

1. Record the complaint in writing.
2. Inform HR immediately.
3. Consider a precautionary suspension while an investigation is arranged (suspension is not a disciplinary sanction).
4. Arrange a confidential meeting with the complainant to gather details.

Policy: “The Company may suspend you pending investigation. Suspension is not a disciplinary sanction.” (Disciplinary Policy – Suspension from work)

Investigation Process

• An impartial investigator is appointed (usually HR).
• Evidence is collected from all parties.
• Findings are recorded and a decision is made within a reasonable time, aiming to avoid “unreasonable delay”.

Policy: “Decision will be given ‘without unreasonable delay’ after a grievance meeting.” (Grievance Procedure)

Outcome and Disciplinary Action

If the investigation confirms harassment, the employee may face gross misconduct sanctions, up to dismissal without notice.

Policy: “Sexual Harassment – Treated as gross misconduct; may result in dismissal without notice.” (Employee Handbook – Conduct and Standards)

Always escalate to HR if:

  • The allegation involves sexual harassment (including third‑party harassment).
  • The conduct could be classified as gross misconduct.
  • The complainant feels the investigation is not proceeding impartially.

Whistleblowing & Protected Disclosure

What counts as a protected disclosure, how to raise one, and what managers must do when they receive one. Victimising someone who has blown the whistle is itself a serious breach — escalate to HR immediately.

What qualifies as a protected disclosure

A protected disclosure is a disclosure of information that an employee reasonably believes shows one or more of the following: a criminal offence, a failure to comply with a legal obligation, a miscarriage of justice, a danger to health or safety, damage to the environment, or the deliberate concealment of any of the above.

Policy: The Company will take all disclosures seriously and investigate appropriately. Employees are protected from detriment or dismissal for making a protected disclosure in good faith. (Employee Handbook — Public Interest Disclosure)

How an employee raises a disclosure

The employee should raise the concern in writing to their line manager, or if the concern involves their line manager, directly to a more senior manager or HR. The disclosure should set out what they believe is happening and why they believe it to be in the public interest to raise it.

Disclosures can also be made to a prescribed person or body (e.g. Ofsted, the Health and Safety Executive) where the employee reasonably believes the Company will not address it.

Your obligations as a manager when you receive a disclosure

If an employee raises a concern that might be a protected disclosure, your obligations are clear:

  • Do not investigate it yourself — escalate to HR immediately
  • Do not discourage the employee from raising it or suggest they are wrong to do so
  • Do not treat them differently, exclude them, or subject them to any detriment because of the disclosure
  • Keep the disclosure confidential — do not discuss it with others
  • Make a written note of what was said and when
Flag: Victimising or dismissing an employee for making a protected disclosure is automatically unfair dismissal, with no qualifying period and no cap on compensation. The risk is severe. Do not attempt to handle this alone.

Protections for the person who raises a concern

An employee who makes a protected disclosure in good faith is protected from any detriment or dismissal as a result. This protection applies even if the disclosure turns out to be unfounded, as long as they had reasonable grounds for believing it to be true.

False or malicious disclosures

Policy: Raising a false allegation or maliciously abusing the whistleblowing procedure can lead to disciplinary action up to and including dismissal without notice for gross misconduct. (Employee Handbook)

Always escalate to HR immediately if:

  • Any employee raises a concern that could be a protected disclosure
  • A disclosure involves a safeguarding concern, criminal activity, or regulatory breach
  • You are the subject of the disclosure, or too close to it
  • You are unsure whether something constitutes a protected disclosure — err on the side of caution
Leave

Time Off for Dependants & Carer's Leave

Two separate rights that are often confused. Time off for dependants covers genuine emergencies. Carer's leave is a planned entitlement for longer-term caring responsibilities. Neither is paid.

Time off for dependants — what it covers

A day-one right for all employees. Covers reasonable unpaid time off to deal with an emergency involving a dependant.

Policy: Permitted reasons include: illness, injury or assault of a dependant; unexpected disruption or breakdown of care arrangements; death of a dependant; unexpected incident involving a child at school. (Employee Handbook — Time Off for Dependants)
A dependant is a spouse, civil partner, child, parent, or anyone who reasonably relies on the employee for care — this includes someone who lives in the same household.

How much time off

There is no set limit in law, but the entitlement is to take a reasonable amount of time — typically enough to deal with the immediate emergency and put alternative arrangements in place. It is not intended to cover extended periods of caring.

Flag: If an employee needs more time than is reasonable to handle an emergency, explore whether annual leave, carer's leave, or unpaid parental leave is more appropriate. Discuss with HR before refusing.

Notice requirements

The employee must tell you as soon as reasonably practicable — they do not need to give advance notice if the emergency arises suddenly. They should tell you the reason for the absence and how long they expect to be away.

Carer's Leave — a separate entitlement

Carer's Leave is a planned entitlement, separate from emergency dependants leave. Employees can take up to one week's unpaid leave per year to provide or arrange care for a dependant with a long-term care need.

Policy (current law): Day-one right. Up to 1 week (pro-rated for part-time workers) per 12-month rolling period. Can be taken as individual days, half-days, or as a block. Employee must give notice of at least twice the amount of leave they want to take (minimum 3 days' notice). The Company can postpone (but not refuse) if the business would be unduly disrupted — must agree an alternative date within one month. (Carer's Leave Act 2023)

Pay

Neither time off for dependants nor carer's leave is paid. Check the individual's contract — there is no Company enhanced entitlement above the statutory minimum for either of these leave types.

Escalate to HR before refusing or challenging if:

  • The employee has a disability or long-term health condition that affects their caring responsibilities — refusal could be indirect discrimination
  • The employee appears to be taking dependants leave repeatedly for what seems like a longer-term caring need — the right approach is a supportive conversation, not a disciplinary one
  • You want to postpone carer's leave — HR should advise on whether postponement is justified
